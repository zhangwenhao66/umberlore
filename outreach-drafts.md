Hi,

I write about art and architectural history at umberlore.com, and I'd like to pitch a guest post for HistoryMedieval on gargoyles, specifically what actually separates one from a grotesque or a chimera.

The short version: a gargoyle is defined by function, not appearance. It's a stone waterspout with a channel cut through it that carries roof water clear of the wall below; a carved figure without that channel is a grotesque, however monstrous it looks. By that definition, most of the famous "gargoyles" on Notre-Dame de Paris, the hybrid creatures along the tower gallery that show up in every postcard, aren't gargoyles at all. The real medieval waterspouts survive mostly on the flying buttresses of the choir, where almost nobody points a camera, and many of those are themselves 19th-century replacements of originals too weathered to save.
