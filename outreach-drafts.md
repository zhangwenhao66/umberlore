The terraces started sagging almost as soon as the formwork came down, and kept sagging for the next 65 years, reaching nearly seven inches of deflection by 1995, all while the house went on being celebrated as a triumph of engineering. The eventual fix, finished in 2002, threaded post-tensioned steel cables through the existing concrete beams and lifted the terraces back up by about three-quarters of an inch, at a cost of $11.5 million, without changing a single visible line of the building. Getting the cables in meant taking up all 557 flagstones in the living room floor first, numbering each one, and resetting them once the work was done.

I'd like to pitch a version of this for DailyArt: not the usual "Fallingwater is a masterpiece" framing, but a look at how close the building came to failing structurally, and what it took to quietly fix it decades later without a visitor today noticing anything. Subject: Guest post idea: most of Notre-Dame's gargoyles aren't gargoyles

Hi,

I write about art and architectural history at umberlore.com, and I'd like to pitch a guest post for HistoryMedieval on gargoyles, specifically what actually separates one from a grotesque or a chimera.

The short version: a gargoyle is defined by function, not appearance. It's a stone waterspout with a channel cut through it that carries roof water clear of the wall below; a carved figure without that channel is a grotesque, however monstrous it looks. By that definition, most of the famous "gargoyles" on Notre-Dame de Paris, the hybrid creatures along the tower gallery that show up in every postcard, aren't gargoyles at all. They're chimeras, added during Viollet-le-Duc and Lassus's 1843-1864 restoration, carved by Victor Pyanet, and they drain nothing. The real medieval waterspouts survive mostly on the flying buttresses of the choir, where almost nobody points a camera, and many of those are themselves 19th-century replacements of originals too weathered to save. I can also work in what happens when a gargoyle's channel actually fails: Reims Cathedral's roof fire in 1914 sent 400 tonnes of molten lead straight through its gargoyles when the roof burned, and the drainage system carried it exactly as designed, just with the wrong fluid.

I'd stick to your word count and cite sources throughout, mostly museum and cathedral conservation records, along with Michael Camille's study of the Notre-Dame restoration. Happy to send a full draft with bio and headshot if you'd like to see the whole piece.
